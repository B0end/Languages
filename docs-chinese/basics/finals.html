Finals

Now that you know the Initials (consonants), let’s look at the other half of the syllable: The Finals.

The Final is the rest of the syllable after the initial consonant. It is built around vowels and can sometimes end with a nasal consonant coda (-n or -ng).

1. The 6 Basic Simple Finals​

FinalIPAMouth Shape & ArticulationExampleMeaning
a[a]Open mouth wide; tongue rests low and flat.啊 āAh! (exclamation)
o[o]Lips rounded into an "O" shape; tongue pulled back.喔 ōOh! (rooster crow)
e[ɤ]Keep tongue in the "o" position, but pull lips back flat into an unrounded smile.鹅 éGoose
i[i]Spread lips into a wide, flat smile; tongue raised high and forward.衣 yīClothes
u[u]Purse lips tightly into a small forward circle; tongue pulled back high.屋 wūHouse, room
ü[y]Keep tongue in the high "i" position, but purse lips into a tight circle like "u".鱼 yúFish

💡 Remember the Standalone Rules from Group 6:

  • i cannot stand alone ➔ written as yi (衣 yī)
  • u cannot stand alone ➔ written as wu (屋 wū)
  • ü cannot stand alone ➔ written as yu with dots dropped (鱼 yú)

2. Compound Finals​

Compound finals are diphthongs and triphthongs: you start at the first vowel and glide smoothly into the next.

💡 Notice the "e" shift: When the letter e is part of a front blend (like ie, üe, ei), it shifts from the throaty "uh" sound to a crisp "eh" or "ay".

3. Nasal Ending Finals (-n vs. -ng)​

The Mechanics:

  • Front Nasal (-n): The tongue tip presses against the gum ridge behind your front upper teeth (just like an English n in sun).
  • Back Nasal (-ng): The back of the tongue rises to seal against the soft palate in the back of the mouth, with no front-teeth contact (like ng in song).

⚠️ Two Critical Pronunciation Traps​

1. The -ian and -üan Vowel Shift ("a" sounds like "e")​

In standard -an, the letter a is an open "ah" sound (like English pan).
However, whenever a is squeezed after i or ü, it automatically shifts into a crisp "eh" sound (like English pen):

  • 天 tiān ➡️ pronounced "tee-en" (rhymes with pen, not pan)
  • 全 quán ➡️ pronounced "ch-ü-en" (rhymes with ü + pen)

2. The -uan Spelling Trap: Real u vs. Secret ü​

Because j, q, x, and y cause the umlaut dots on ü to be dropped, the written letters -uan represent two completely different sounds depending on the initial consonant:

4. The "ü" Dot Rule (When to Drop vs. When to Keep)​

🧠 The Linguistic Logic:

  • j, q, x, y: Physically cannot pair with the regular u ("oo") sound in Mandarin. Because there is zero risk of confusion, Pinyin drops the dots (ju, qu, xu, yu).
  • n and l: Can pair with both regular u and umlaut ü. Therefore, the dots must be kept to distinguish completely different words (nu vs. nü, lu vs. lü).
InitialWritten PinyinActual SoundThe Dot RuleExampleMeaning

xxux + ü❌ Drop dots需 xūTo need
yyuü❌ Drop dots雨 yǔRain
nnün + ü✅ Keep dots女 nǚWoman
llül + ü✅ Keep dots绿 lǜGreen

Why N and L Need the Dots (Minimal Pair Contrast)​

If the dots were dropped for n and l, you would not be able to tell these words apart:

💡 Bonus Typing Trick:
Standard keyboards do not have a ü key. To type 女 (nǚ) or 绿 (lǜ), use the letter v:

  • Type nv ➔ 女
  • Type lv ➔ 绿

5. All Sounds of Pinyin "E"​

⚡ The 3-Second Rule for "E":

  • By itself: Say "UH" (deep/throaty) ➔ 哥 gē, 喝 hē, 的 de
  • In a front blend: Say "EH" or "AY" ➔ 谢 xiè, 月 yuè, 北 běi
  • Followed by "r": Say "ERR" (tongue curled) ➔ 二 èr

6. The "Hidden E" Finals (Spelling Abbreviation Rules)​

In Pinyin orthography, the letter e is omitted in spelling whenever an initial consonant is added, but the vowel sound remains fully pronounced.

The Two "Hidden E" Rules​
