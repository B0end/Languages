# The 4 Tones and Tone Changes

In Mandarin Chinese, **pitch contour changes the word entirely**. 

Changing your pitch is not used to convey emotion or emphasis—it selects an entirely different dictionary entry with a completely different Chinese character and meaning.

---

## The 5 Tones Benchmark (The "Ma" Scale)

| Tone | Contour <br /> *(Scale 1–5)* | Pitch Action | Example | Meaning | Vocal Anchor / Memory Cue |
| :--- | :---: | :--- | :---: | :--- | :--- |
| **1st Tone** <br /> *(High Level)* | **55** ( ̄ ) | High, flat, and sustained | [妈 mā](ma1.wav) | Mother | High singing note / saying *"Ahhh"* for the doctor |
| **2nd Tone** <br /> *(Rising)* | **35** ( ˊ ) | Smooth rise from mid to high | [麻 má](ma2.wav) | Hemp | Asking a surprised question: *"Huh?!"* / *"What?!"* |
| **3rd Tone** <br /> *(Dipping / Low)* | **214** ( ˇ ) | Dips to bottom, then rises | [马 mǎ](ma3.wav) | Horse | Low hesitation grunt (*"Uhh..."*); vocal fry |
| **4th Tone** <br /> *(Falling)* | **51** ( ˋ ) | Sharp drop from top to bottom | [骂 mà](ma4.wav) | To scold | Decisive, firm command: *"No!"* / a karate chop |
| **Neutral Tone** <br /> *(De-stressed)* | **—** *(No mark)* | Short, light, and unstressed | [吗 ma](ma0.wav) | *(Question)* | Effortless exhale; like the second syllable of *sofa* |

---

> 💡 **The #1 Real-World Tip for the 3rd Tone (Half 3rd Tone):**  
> In isolation or at the end of a sentence, the 3rd tone dips and rises (**214**).  
> However, in **fast, natural speech before any other tone**, native speakers **do not rise**—they pronounce only the first half: a low, flat pitch (**21**). Thinking of it as a **low tone** rather than a "rollercoaster" makes speaking fluent Mandarin much easier!

---

### 2. Ear-Training: Tone 1 (55 ➡️) vs. Tone 4 (51 ↘️)

Beginners frequently confuse Tone 1 and Tone 4 because **both begin at the very top of your vocal pitch (Level 5)**:
* **Tone 1:** Stays high, flat, and sustained like a singer holding a steady note.
* **Tone 4:** Plummets aggressively straight downward like a firm exclamation mark.

| Syllable | Tone 1: High Level (55) | Meaning | Tone 4: Sharp Falling (51) | Meaning |
| :---: | :---: | :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **ba** | [八 bā](ba1.wav) | Eight | [爸 bà](ba4.wav) | Dad |
| **shi** | [师 shī](shi1.wav) | Teacher | [是 shì](shi4.wav) | To be, yes |
| **fei** | [飞 fēi](fei1.wav) | To fly | [费 fèi](fei4.wav) | Fee, cost |
| **zhu** | [猪 zhū](zhu1.wav) | Pig | [住 zhù](zhu4.wav) | To live |

### 3. High-Stakes Tone Pairs (The "Danger Zone")

Because tone determines the word, an inaccurate pitch can turn a polite conversation into an awkward situation:

| Contrast Pair | Word A | Meaning | Word B | Meaning | The Social Blunder |
| :--- | :---: | :--- | :---: | :--- | :--- |
| **Ask vs. Kiss** | [问 wèn](wen4.wav) | To ask *(4th)* | [吻 wěn](wen3.wav) | To kiss *(3rd)* | Saying *“Qǐng wěn...”* (请吻 - **"Please kiss me"**) instead of *“Qǐng wèn...”* (请问 - **"May I ask..."**). |
| **Buy vs. Sell** | [买 mǎi](mai3.wav) | To buy *(3rd)* | [卖 mài](mai4.wav) | To sell *(4th)* | Confusing whether you want to purchase an item or demand payment at a market. |
| **Sleep vs. Dumplings** | [睡觉 shuìjiào](shuijiao4.wav) | To sleep *(4th+4th)* | [水饺 shuǐjiǎo](shuijiao3.wav) | Dumplings *(3rd+3rd)* | Telling a restaurant waiter: *“Wǒ xiǎng shuìjiào”* (**"I want to sleep"**) instead of ordering dumplings. |
| **Panda vs. Chest Hair** | [熊猫 xióngmāo](xion2mao1.wav) | Panda *(2nd+1st)* | [胸毛 xiōngmáo](xion1mao2.wav) | Chest hair *(1st+2nd)* | Telling your host family that you came to China to see the **chest hair**! |

### 4. Where to Place the Tone Mark? (The Vowel Hierarchy)

When writing Pinyin, the tone mark always sits above a vowel. Placement follows a strict priority order based on **mouth aperture** (how wide your mouth opens):

a -> o -> e -> i -> u -> ü

#### The 3-Step Placement Checklist:
1. **Always look for `a` first:** If **`a`** is present, it *always* takes the mark:
   * *m**ā***, *xi**ǎ**o*, *hu**à**n*
2. **Next, look for `o` or `e`:** If there is no **`a`**, the mark goes on **`o`** or **`e`**:
   * *g**ǒ**u*, *h**ē**i*, *yu**è***
3. **The "iu / ui" Tie-Breaker:** When **`i`** and **`u`** appear side-by-side, the mark **always goes on the second (last) vowel**:
   * In **iu** ➔ goes on **`u`**: [六 liù](liu4.wav), [九 jiǔ](jiu3.wav)
   * In **ui** ➔ goes on **`i`**: [水 shuǐ](shui3.wav), [对 duì](dui4.wav)

## Tone Sandhi & Tone Changes

In English, we naturally say *"don't"* instead of *"do not"* and *"an apple"* instead of *"a apple"* to create a smoother vocal flow. 

Mandarin does the exact same thing with tones: **when certain tones collide in natural speech, they shift automatically to make pronunciation effortless and smooth.**

---

### ⚡ Master Tone Sandhi Matrix

| Syllable | Followed by 4th Tone (↘️) | Followed by 1st / 2nd / Neutral | Followed by 3rd Tone (⤵️) | Standalone / Counting |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **3rd Tone** (⤵️⤴️) | **Half-3rd** *(Low ⤵️)* | **Half-3rd** *(Low ⤵️)* | **2nd Tone** *(Rise ↗️)* | **Full 3rd** *(Dip & rise ⤵️⤴️)* |
| **不 (bù)** | **bú** *(2nd Tone ↗️)* | **bù** *(4th Tone ↘️)* | **bù** *(4th Tone ↘️)* | **bù** *(4th Tone ↘️)* |
| **一 (yī)** | **yí** *(2nd Tone ↗️)* | **yì** *(4th Tone ↘️)* | **yì** *(4th Tone ↘️)* | **yī** *(1st Tone ➡️)* |

### 1. The 3rd Tone Shape-Shifter

The textbook 3rd tone takes significant effort to fully dip down and rise back up. In real speech, it adjusts automatically:

| Environment | Tone Rule | Example | Spoken As | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **3rd + 3rd Tone** | The 1st word bounces up to a **2nd Tone (↗️)** | [你好 nǐ hǎo](ni3hao3.wav)<br />[水果 shuǐguǒ](shui3guo3.wav)<br />[手表 shǒubiǎo](shou3biao3.wav) | **ní** hǎo<br />**shuí** guǒ<br />**shóu** biǎo | Hello<br />Fruit<br />Wristwatch |
| **Before 1st, 2nd, 4th, or Neutral** | Drops to a **Half-3rd** <br />*(Low flat pitch ⤵️; never rises)* | [老师 lǎoshī](lao3shi1.wav)<br />[很忙 hěnmáng](hen3mang2.wav)<br />[很大 hěndà](hen3da4.wav)<br />[喜欢 xǐhuan](xi3huan0.wav) | **lǎo**-shī *(Low + High)*<br />**hěn**-máng *(Low + Rise)*<br />**hěn**-dà *(Low + Drop)*<br />**xǐ**-huan *(Low + Light)* | Teacher<br />Very busy<br />Very big<br />To like |
| **Alone / End of Sentence** | Stays a **Full 3rd Tone** <br />*(Full dip and rise ⤵️⤴️)* | [很好 hěnhǎo](hen3hao3.wav) | hén **hǎo** | Very good |

> 💡 **What about 3 Third Tones in a Row (e.g., 我很好)?**  
> Group the words by grammatical chunk:  
> * **[我] + [很好]** (*wǒ* + *hěn hǎo*)  
> * `[wǒ (Half-3rd ⤵️)]` + `[hén (2nd ↗️)]` + `[hǎo (Full 3rd ⤵️⤴️)]` ➔ [我很好 wǒ hén hǎo](wo3hen3hao3.wav)

### 2. The "Anti-Double-Chop" Rule: 不 (*bù*)

* **Default Base Tone:** **bù** (4th Tone ↘️ — Sharp Falling Tone)

Pronouncing two 4th tones in a row (*bù + shì*) feels abrupt and jarring. Therefore, whenever **不** is followed by another **4th tone**, it automatically bounces up to a **2nd tone (bú ↗️)**:

| Environment | Spoken Tone | Example | Spoken As | Meaning |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **Before a 4th Tone (↘️)** <br />*(Tone Shift)* | **bú** <br />*(2nd Tone ↗️)* | [不是 bù shì](bu4shi4.wav)<br />[不对 bù duì](bu4dui4.wav)<br />[不要 bù yào](bu4yao4.wav)<br />[不客气 bù kèqi](bu4ke4qi0.wav) | **bú** shì<br />**bú** duì<br />**bú** yào<br />**bú** kèqi | Is not / No<br />Incorrect<br />Don't want<br />You're welcome |
| **Before 1st, 2nd, or 3rd Tone** <br />*(Default Base Tone)* | **bù** <br />*(4th Tone ↘️)* | [不吃 bù chī](bu4chi1.wav)<br />[不行 bù xíng](bu4xing2.wav)<br />[不好 bù hǎo](bu4hao3.wav) | **bù** chī<br />**bù** xíng<br />**bù** hǎo | Do not eat<br />Not okay<br />Not good |

---

:::note

A common point of confusion for learners: **Why don't Pinyin marks in dictionaries always match the tones native speakers use?**

* **3rd Tone Sandhi (`nǐ hǎo`):** Dictionaries and textbooks **always preserve the original base tone** in print (`nǐ hǎo`). The speaker is expected to apply the 3rd-tone sandhi rule automatically and pronounce `ní hǎo`.
* **不 (*bù*) and 一 (*yī*):** Modern learner materials frequently print the *actual changed tone* (e.g., `yí gè`, `bú shì`) to guide pronunciation. However, formal Chinese dictionaries list each character under its dictionary base tone (`yī`, `bù`).
:::
