The "R", "S/Z", and "J/G" Consonants

1. The "R" Sound: Forget the Spanish Trill​

In standard Brazilian Portuguese, you never roll your tongue at the front of your mouth for an initial R or a double RR.

Instead, the sound moves back to your throat, sounding like the English "H" in hotel (or a very soft Latin American Spanish "J" in jamón, without the harsh throat rasp).

2. "S" vs. "Z": The Vibrating Bee Sound​

Spanish only has a voiceless S (the vocal cords do not vibrate: casa, mesa, sol).

Portuguese has two distinct phonemes:

  • Voiceless /s/: No vocal vibration (like the hiss of a snake).
  • Voiced /z/: The vocal cords must buzz (like a bee buzzing).

When to Make the "Z" Buzz:​

  1. Whenever the letter Z appears: zebra, fazer.
  2. Whenever a single S is between two vowels: casa, mesa, música.

:::caution The Spanish Trap: "Casa" In Spanish, you say "ca-sa". In Portuguese, you must say "ca-za". If you use a voiceless S, you are saying cassa (from the verb cassar, to annul/revoke). :::

3. "J" and "G" (Before E/I): Stop Scratching the Throat​

In Spanish, J and G (before e/i) are voiceless velar fricatives: a raspy, friction-heavy sound produced in the back of the throat (gente, jamón, jefe).

In Portuguese, this sound does not exist. Instead, J and G (before e/i) are pronounced like the "s" in the English word measure or the French "J" in bonjour (/ʒ/).

  • The mouth position is soft.
  • The tongue is forward near the palate.
  • The vocal cords vibrate smoothly with zero throat rasp.
